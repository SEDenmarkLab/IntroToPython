# An Introduction to Python for the Denmark Lab

### Developed by Blake E. Ocampo

[S. E. Denmark Laboratory, University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign](https://denmarkgroup.illinois.edu/)

The following repository represents a small tutorial to some of the basics of Python. The goal here is solely to act as an on boarding to new students, especially chemists/people that are less familiar with a lot of the concepts. The goal of this is to provide some of the key questions and takeaways to understand the structure of Python, ranging from native types, key language, and key functionality that exists that might be beneficial as you are beginning to develop your own code.

There are plenty of starting points and tutorials out there online, but it can be somewhat overwhelming as to where to start and what might be some of the most important areas to cover for any applications in our group (or others). 

### Note: THIS IS NOT AN OFFICIAL PYTHON COURSE OR NECESSARILY GOOD PEDAGOGICAL TRAINING. This is based off of personal experience and pitfalls that we fell often fell into when joining the group.

The only purpose is to get you standing on your own to be able to do some sort of applied research, which means it will not always be the best practices since we are chemists applying computer science, not the other way around. If you would like to learn the true fundamentals (which I'd highly recommend), you should consider looking at the Python course offered at UC Berkeley as a great starting point You can find that [here](https://cs61a.org/fa26/).

### Note 2: This is not going to give you all the answers, the goal is for you to research and answer for yourself the questions provided. 

What you are going to be doing is copying the template provided here into your own branch, fill out the questions and exercises as you do them, and then share them when you are ready for review. We can add separate tutorials from there. That means this is an "autodidactic" or self-driven exercise, where you have to find the answers yourself and check with people that may know.

## Ground Rules:
* You can use search tools and resources available online, such as Google, StackExchange, and even LLMs to get descriptions regarding the individual topics or researching some of the key aspects of the units.
* You **_ARE NOT_** allowed  to use any packages in your answers outside of those initially installed in your environment unless told explicitly. For example, you are allowed to use the "math" import, but you are not allowed to install packages such as `numpy` or `pandas` unless explicitly stated.
  * Python packages are meant to help accelerate all the monotonous things, and you will use plenty of them in the future. For initial learning of Python, it is best to learn how native Python works so you can critically think about how one implements solutions rather than take all the advantages.
* You **_ARE NOT_** allowed to use LLMs during the exercises, but you are more than free to use Google (no AI), StackExchange, or any other resources to research the methods and functionality that is available to you.
  * LLMs are inescapable nowadays, and they have the ability to dominate a majority of the workflows and coding you create. They will be invaluable to you as you begin and continue through your coding journey. They will also absolutely give you the correct answer for the exercises. However, it will severely limit your ability to think about Python and how different designing solutions are from the way you are used to. It's in your benefit to learn to exercise the side of your brain you're not used to.
